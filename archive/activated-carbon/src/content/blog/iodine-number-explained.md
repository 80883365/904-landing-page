If you have ever compared activated-carbon datasheets, you have seen the **iodine number**. It is the most commonly cited figure 鈥?and one of the most commonly misunderstood.

## What the iodine number measures

The iodine number quantifies how much iodine (in milligrams) one gram of carbon adsorbs from solution under standard test conditions (typically ASTM D4607). Because iodine is a **small molecule**, it accesses the finest (micropore) structure of the carbon. The result is a proxy for the carbon's **micropore volume** 鈥?and therefore its capacity for other small molecules.

## A higher number is not always better

A carbon with an iodine number of 1100 mg/g has more micropore surface area than one rated at 850 mg/g. That is genuinely useful **if your target contaminant is small** (trace organics in drinking water, gold cyanide complexes). But if you are trying to remove large, colored molecules 鈥?say, decolorizing sugar syrup 鈥?a high iodine number tells you almost nothing useful. There you want a high **methylene blue** number, which reflects mesopore capacity.

## Watch the test, not just the number

Iodine number is sensitive to the test method, particle size, and even moisture. When comparing suppliers, make sure:

1. The **same standard** was used (ASTM D4607 vs AWWA).
2. The carbon was tested at a **consistent mesh size**.
3. Moisture content was accounted for (results are reported **on a dry basis**).

## The bottom line

Treat the iodine number as one signal among several. Pair it with methylene blue (mesopores), CTC (gas phase), hardness, and ash to build a complete picture. And always validate with a **bench-scale test on your actual stream** before committing to a bulk purchase.
